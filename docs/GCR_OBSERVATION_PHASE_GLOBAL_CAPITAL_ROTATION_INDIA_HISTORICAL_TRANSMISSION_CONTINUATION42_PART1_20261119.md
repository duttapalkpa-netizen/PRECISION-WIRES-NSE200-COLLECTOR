# Global Capital Rotation — Observation Phase: Historical transmission into India
Date: 2026-11-19
Continuation: 42, Part-1
Research stage: OBSERVATION ONLY. Planning and organizing are deliberately not yet treated as completed.
Scope: evidence-led historical synthesis of how global capital, liquidity, risk and trade/commodity shocks transmit into India's external accounts, FX, rates, sectors and equity market. This is not a claim that every country must be included as an independent panel member.

## 1. Research question and boundary
Question: through which measurable channels have global financial and real-economy shocks influenced India's capital flows, rupee, domestic yields, liquidity, corporate cash flows and equity returns since external-sector liberalisation?
Unit of analysis is not "all countries equally". It is the transmission channel and the source/market that materially changes India's marginal funding, demand, commodity bill, risk premium or portfolio allocation. Country coverage should later be evidence-weighted by these channels.
This document is a historical observation register, not a causal model, trading signal, weight set, or completed GCR design.

## 2. Evidence-backed historical observations

### A. Structural opening and composition shift (1991–2002)
- RBI's historical account records that before 1991 India depended more on external assistance, NRI deposits and commercial borrowings; after reforms, foreign investment became more important and the composition shifted toward non-debt-creating flows.
- FPIs were first permitted to invest in Indian equities on 14 September 1992, subject to restrictions; liberalisation over time increased the integration of equity and FX markets.
- Implication to test, not assume: the post-reform India transmission system must distinguish debt, FDI, portfolio equity, portfolio debt, NRI deposits, ECB, trade credit and reserve movements. A single "capital flow" series hides different risk and maturity profiles.

### B. Global liquidity boom and GFC reversal (2003–2009)
- RBI research describes capital-flow acceleration from 2003, with accommodative advanced-economy monetary conditions as a push factor, peaking around Q4 2007, followed by the 2008–09 GFC retrenchment/sudden stop.
- RBI research also finds that the 2008 GFC outflow was concentrated more in equity portfolio flows, whereas 2013 taper volatility affected debt flows more strongly in some episodes.
- Observation: the same headline global shock can hit different Indian instruments differently; equity FPI, debt FPI, ECB, trade credit and bank flows need separate lanes.

### C. Post-GFC QE, taper tantrum and China shock (2010–2016)
- RBI documents recovery in EME flows after 2010 under advanced-economy asset purchases and low policy rates; flows declined during 2013–15 after Fed taper/normalisation communication.
- RBI's sudden-stop study notes that taper episodes were not necessarily classified as full sudden stops; the 2013 episode was often a "sudden pause". Financially developed and liquid EMEs with larger inflows faced stronger exchange-rate/reserve/equity pressure.
- RBI identifies the 2015 Chinese equity sell-off and renminbi devaluation as additional contributors to moderation in EME capital flows; however, its panel event study did not find a statistically significant fall in aggregate EME net FPI flows attributable to the 2016 China sell-off. Do not convert this into a presumed India-specific causal effect.
- Observation: event labels are not enough; each event requires India-specific flow/price response and cross-country controls.

### D. Pandemic shock and liquidity rebound (2020–2021)
- RBI finds exceptionally large, rapid FPI reversals in early 2020 during flight to safety; later, very accommodative advanced-economy policies supported a rebound in EME flows in late-2020/2021.
- RBI's study says pandemic and GFC immediate impacts on weekly EME net FPI were particularly strong, and pandemic effects persisted longer than other episodes in its sample.
- Observation: speed, persistence and reversal asymmetry matter; daily/weekly FPI and market data should complement quarterly BoP data.

### E. Synchronous tightening, commodity and geopolitical shocks (2022)
- RBI includes Russia–Ukraine war and synchronised aggressive advanced-economy tightening in the major reversal episodes affecting EME weekly FPI flows.
- Mechanisms requiring separate measurement for India: crude/energy import bill and inflation; USD funding and global yields; risk-off portfolio selling; INR and RBI FX intervention/reserves; domestic bond yields and equity sector margins.
- These are candidate channels; their individual causal contributions must be estimated rather than inferred from temporal coincidence.

### F. Domestic resilience versus global flow pressure (2023–2026)
- RBI Annual Report 2025-26 reports net FPI to India of +US$44.6bn in FY2023-24, +US$3.3bn in FY2024-25, and -US$16.5bn in FY2025-26 (the FY2025-26 figure is sourced from NSDL, unlike prior BoP-based years).
- SEBI Annual Report 2025-26 reports total FPI net outflow of ₹1,52,692 crore in FY2025-26, compared with +₹3,39,066 crore in FY2023-24 and +₹20,018 crore in FY2024-25. It separately reports equity outflow ₹1,80,832 crore and debt inflow ₹25,807 crore in FY2025-26. This is a crucial composition divergence: total FPI flow is not equity flow.
- SEBI attributes the FY2025-26 equity outflows to a combination of US tariff uncertainty, West Asia geopolitical tensions, subdued corporate earnings due to higher input costs and medium/long-term inflation uncertainty. Treat these as SEBI's stated drivers, not a complete causal decomposition.
- RBI's 2026 annual report also shows net FDI +US$7.7bn in FY2025-26, with gross inward FDI US$94.5bn and repatriation/disinvestment US$53.6bn; net FPI -US$16.5bn. FDI and FPI therefore moved in different directions and must not be collapsed into one flow score.

## 3. Transmission map observed in official research
1. Global monetary/risk push: advanced-economy policy rates, long yields, global liquidity, volatility/risk appetite → EME portfolio gross/net flows.
2. Portfolio/FX loop: FPI buys Indian assets → sells FX/buys INR; disinvestment reverses the transaction. RBI research documents synchronous stock/forex market interaction, while also finding that spillover direction and volatility depend on regime.
3. Trade/terms-of-trade channel: crude/energy and other import prices → import bill/current account/inflation → INR, policy expectations, corporate margins and sector dispersion.
4. Funding/balance-sheet channel: USD rates and INR depreciation → foreign-currency debt service, refinancing cost and imported-input costs; exporters and importers experience different cash-flow effects.
5. Domestic pull/resilience: GDP growth, earnings, valuations, domestic institutional/mutual-fund flows, market depth, reserves, CAD and policy response can amplify or offset global push.
6. Cross-border contagion: global risk events transmit via gross inflows/outflows and market repricing, not only net flows. Netting can conceal simultaneous large inflows and outflows.

## 4. Critical methodological observations
- Do not treat all countries as equally relevant or require all countries' macro series before India overlay can be useful. Build a transmission graph: global source → price/flow/financing channel → India variable → Indian sector/market response.
- Country inclusion should be based on measurable exposure: funding currency/financial centre, investor domicile, major trade/commodity supplier, global demand partner, regional contagion benchmark, or systemic market.
- Separate push vs pull factors, gross vs net flows, FDI vs FPI, equity vs debt, resident vs non-resident, transactions vs positions, USD vs INR, and quarterly vs daily/weekly frequency.
- Avoid causal claims from simple correlation. Use event studies, local projections/panel models, rolling betas, lead-lag analysis, and out-of-sample tests; control for India domestic news and global common shocks.
- Build regime-specific relationships: correlations and transmission coefficients are not stable across crises, liquidity regimes, capital controls, and domestic policy response.
- Preserve source vintage and revisions. Current-vintage historical series cannot be represented as the information set available to traders at the time.
- A quarterly BoP panel is a slow structural context layer; it cannot alone generate a 1–3 day trading signal. High-frequency market/flow proxies are needed for timing, with explicit timestamp and latency controls.
